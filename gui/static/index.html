    How to use this

    1. Add the artefact. Drop the file you were given on the left panel. A packet capture, a firmware or cartridge image, an old executable, a log - the interface identifies it by content, not by its name.
    2. Read the identification card. It says in plain words what the file is, how much traffic it holds, which industrial protocols are inside, and warns you about things that will bite you later (IP fragmentation, a tampered checksum, data appended past the end of a binary).
    3. Work down the suggested steps. They are ordered: orientation first, then the protocol-specific report, then the narrow views that answer "what was changed" and "did the device accept it". The highlighted button is the recommended next click.
    4. Read "What this means" under each result. The tables are precise but assume protocol knowledge; that block translates them and tells you what to look at next.
    5. Keep the command lines. Every result shows the exact command that produced it. Copy them into your notes - they are your audit trail, and running them yourself is how you stop needing the GUI.

    What the analysis is actually looking for

    Industrial protocols were designed for reliability on a trusted wire, not for authentication. Almost every finding in this toolkit comes down to four questions:

    • Who spoke? Control traffic is repetitive and goes to fixed peers. A host that contacts many devices, or asks questions without ever answering any, does not belong.
    • Did it read or did it write? Reads are monitoring. Writes - a coil forced, a setpoint changed, a code block downloaded - change the physical process.
    • Did the device accept it? Every protocol here answers differently for success and refusal. Refusals are evidence too: they show what the intruder tried and could not do.
    • In what order? Attackers silence reporting first, then act. A timeline usually tells the story better than any single packet.
